Elasticities

How strongly does the quantity demanded react when a price or income changes? Elasticities answer in percentages, so they don't depend on the units.

Own-price elasticity of demand

The own-price elasticity is the percentage change in quantity demanded per 1% change in the good's own price:

\[ \varepsilon=\frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P}\;\longrightarrow\;\varepsilon=\frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}\quad\text{(at a point)}. \]

For an ordinary good \(\varepsilon<0\). We classify by its magnitude:

\(|\varepsilon|\)CalledIf the price rises, total spending \(PQ\)…
\(>1\)elasticfalls
\(=1\)unit elasticstays the same
\(<1\)inelasticrises
Common trap

Elasticity is not the slope. A straight-line demand curve has a constant slope but its elasticity changes along it: it is very elastic at high prices (small \(Q\)) and inelastic at low prices (large \(Q\)).

Linear demand and spending slide the price along \(Q=a-bP\)

Cross-price elasticity

How does the demand for \(a\) respond to the price of a different good \(b\)?

\[ \varepsilon_{ab}=\frac{\%\Delta Q_a}{\%\Delta p_b}=\frac{\partial Q_a}{\partial p_b}\cdot\frac{p_b}{Q_a}. \]
  • \(\varepsilon_{ab}>0\): \(a\) and \(b\) are substitutes. A pricier \(b\) sends buyers to \(a\) (coffee and tea).
  • \(\varepsilon_{ab}<0\): \(a\) and \(b\) are complements. A pricier \(b\) reduces demand for \(a\) (coffee and cream).
  • \(\varepsilon_{ab}=0\): unrelated, as with Cobb-Douglas.

Income elasticity

How does demand respond to a change in income \(Y\)?

\[ \eta=\frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta Y}=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial Y}\cdot\frac{Y}{Q}. \]
  • \(\eta<0\): inferior good. Demand falls as income rises (instant noodles, bus rides).
  • \(\eta>0\): normal good. If \(0<\eta<1\) it is a necessity (its budget share falls as income rises); if \(\eta>1\) it is a luxury (its budget share rises).

Worked exercises

All three elasticities at a point

Demand for \(a\) is \(Q_a=50-4p_a+2p_b+0.5Y\). At \(p_a=5\), \(p_b=10\), \(Y=40\), find the own-price, cross-price and income elasticities and classify the goods.

Show solution

\(Q_a=50-20+20+20=70\).

Own: \(\varepsilon=-4\cdot\frac{5}{70}=-\frac27\approx-0.29\): inelastic.
Cross: \(\varepsilon_{ab}=2\cdot\frac{10}{70}=\frac27\approx0.29>0\): substitutes.
Income: \(\eta=0.5\cdot\frac{40}{70}=\frac27\approx0.29\): normal good and a necessity (\(0<\eta<1\)).

Revenue-maximizing price

Demand is \(Q=120-4P\). At what price is total spending largest, and what is the elasticity there?

Show solution

Spending \(R=P(120-4P)=120P-4P^2\). Setting \(R'=120-8P=0\) gives \(P^*=15\), \(Q^*=60\), \(R^*=900\).

Elasticity: \(\varepsilon=-4\cdot\frac{15}{60}=-1\). Spending is maximized exactly where demand is unit elastic: below \(P=15\) it is inelastic (raising the price helps), above it is elastic (raising the price hurts).

Elasticity from utility

A consumer has \(u(a,b)=\sqrt a+b\). Her interior demand for \(a\) is \(D_a=\left(\dfrac{p_b}{2p_a}\right)^2\). Find the own-price, cross-price and income elasticities of demand for \(a\).

Show solution

Write \(D_a=\frac{p_b^2}{4}p_a^{-2}\). For a power function \(D=Kx^k\), the elasticity with respect to \(x\) is just the exponent \(k\):

  • own price: exponent of \(p_a\) is \(-2\), so \(\varepsilon=-2\) (elastic);
  • cross price: exponent of \(p_b\) is \(+2\), so \(\varepsilon_{ab}=+2\) (substitutes);
  • income: \(Y\) doesn't appear, so \(\eta=0\) (all extra income goes to \(b\)).
