Budget sets

What can the consumer afford? Before worrying about what she wants, we pin down what she can buy.

The market assumptions

  • Constant (linear) prices: the price per unit doesn't change with the amount bought. No quantity discounts.
  • Infinitely divisible goods: 1.5 units is fine, so the budget line is a smooth line.
  • Price takers with income \(Y\): the consumer cannot change prices and spends at most \(Y\).

The budget set

With two goods \(a\) and \(b\), prices \(p_a, p_b>0\) and income \(Y\), the budget set is every nonnegative bundle she can pay for:

\[ B=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb R_+^2:\ p_a a+p_b b\le Y\}. \]

Its upper edge, \(p_a a+p_b b=Y\), is the budget line. Solving for \(b\) gives \(b=\dfrac{Y}{p_b}-\dfrac{p_a}{p_b}\,a\): vertical intercept \(Y/p_b\), horizontal intercept \(Y/p_a\), slope \(-p_a/p_b\).

Interpretation

The price ratio \(p_a/p_b\) is the market's relative value of the two goods: to buy one more unit of \(a\) you must give up \(p_a/p_b\) units of \(b\).

Things to try

  1. Raise \(p_a\) only. Which intercept moves? (The pivot happens around the \(b\)-intercept.)
  2. Double income. The line shifts out in parallel: the slope \(-p_a/p_b\) doesn't involve \(Y\).
  3. Double everything: both prices and income. Where does the line end up?
Common trap

Only relative prices and real income matter. Multiplying \(p_a\), \(p_b\) and \(Y\) by the same number leaves the budget set, and therefore the optimal bundles, unchanged.

Worked exercise

Handout 2, Question 2

Suppose \(p_a=\$2\), \(p_b=\$4\) and \(Y=\$20\).

  1. What is the budget set?
  2. What happens to the budget set if \(p_a\) increases to $4?
  3. What happens if income doubles under the original prices?
  4. What happens if income and both prices double from their original values?
Show solution
  1. \(B=\{(a,b)\ge0:\ 2a+4b\le20\}\). Intercepts: \((10,0)\) and \((0,5)\); slope \(-1/2\).
  2. \(4a+4b\le20\). Intercepts \((5,0)\) and \((0,5)\); slope \(-1\). The set shrinks: the \(b\)-intercept is unchanged and the \(a\)-intercept falls from 10 to 5. Anything with a lot of \(a\) became unaffordable.
  3. \(2a+4b\le40\). Intercepts \((20,0)\) and \((0,10)\); slope still \(-1/2\). The set expands, with a parallel shift outward.
  4. \(4a+8b\le40\), which divides by 2 to \(2a+4b\le20\): the same budget set as originally. Nothing changes.
