Substitution & income effects

When the price of a good changes, the consumer reacts for two different reasons. Separating them explains why demand curves slope down, and when they might not.

Two reasons to change your purchases

Suppose the price of \(a\) falls. Two things happen at once:

  • Substitution effect (SE): \(a\) is now relatively cheaper than \(b\), so she shifts toward \(a\). We isolate this by holding her real income fixed.
  • Income effect (IE): the lower price means she can afford more overall: her real income rose. She changes purchases of both goods accordingly, depending on whether \(a\) is normal or inferior.
\[ \underbrace{a_C-a_A}_{\text{total effect}}=\underbrace{a_B-a_A}_{\text{substitution effect}}+\underbrace{a_C-a_B}_{\text{income effect}}. \]

Here \(A\) is the original optimum, \(C\) is the final optimum, and \(B\) is an imaginary intermediate bundle that has the new relative prices but the old real income.

Finding the intermediate bundle

To separate the two effects we ask: what would she buy at the new relative prices if her utility stayed exactly where it was? That bundle is \(B\).

  1. Find the original optimum \(A\) and its utility \(u_0\).
  2. Find \(B\) on the original indifference curve \(u=u_0\), at the point where its slope equals the new price ratio: \(|\mathrm{MRS}_{ab}|=p_a^{new}/p_b\). Equivalently, \(B\) is the cheapest bundle at the new prices that still gives \(u_0\). The line through \(B\) with the new slope is the compensated budget line: parallel to the new budget line and just touching the original indifference curve.
  3. Find the final optimum \(C\) at the new prices and her actual income.

Then SE is the move \(A\to B\) and IE is the move \(B\to C\).

The signs

Price of \(a\) falls…Substitution effectIncome effectTotal
\(a\) normal\(+\)\(+\)\(+\) (reinforce)
\(a\) inferior\(+\)\(-\)usually \(+\) (SE bigger)
\(a\) Giffen\(+\)\(-\), bigger than SE\(-\) (demand slopes up!)

The SE always goes against the price change: price falls \(\Rightarrow\) buy more of \(a\) (price rises \(\Rightarrow\) buy less). The IE's sign matches the income elasticity: for a price fall, a normal good (\(\eta>0\)) rises and an inferior good (\(\eta<0\)) falls. Everything flips for a price rise.

Things to try

  • Cobb-Douglas: both effects are positive for a price fall. Which effect is larger when the price drops a lot?
  • Perfect complements: where is \(B\)? The substitution effect is exactly zero.
  • Perfect substitutes (try \(\alpha=\beta=1\) with prices crossing \(p_a=p_b\)): she jumps from one corner to the other.
  • Quasilinear: good \(a\) has no income effect. All of the change is substitution.
  • Make the new price higher than the old. Check that the SE reduces consumption of \(a\).

Worked exercises

Cobb-Douglas

A consumer has \(u(a,b)=ab\), income \(Y=48\) and \(p_b=2\). The price of \(a\) falls from \(4\) to \(1\). Find the substitution and income effects on \(a\).

Show solution

Start and end. Cobb-Douglas with equal exponents spends half of income on each good. At \(p_a=4\): \(a_A=\frac{48}{2\cdot4}=6\), \(b_A=\frac{48}{2\cdot2}=12\), so \(u_0=72\). At \(p_a=1\): \(a_C=\frac{48}{2\cdot1}=24\). Total effect \(=24-6=18\).

Intermediate bundle. Stay on \(u=72\) and find the tangency at the new price ratio: \(|MRS|=\frac ba=\frac{p_a}{p_b}=\frac12\Rightarrow b=\frac a2\). Then \(a\cdot\frac a2=72\Rightarrow a_B=12\).

Effects. SE \(=a_B-a_A=12-6=6\). IE \(=a_C-a_B=24-12=12\). Both are positive (normal good), and SE + IE \(=18\).

Perfect complements

A consumer has \(u(a,b)=\min\{a,b\}\), \(Y=24\) and \(p_b=2\). The price of \(a\) falls from 6 to 2. Find the SE and IE on \(a\).

Show solution

She always buys \(a=b\). Budget: \((p_a+p_b)a=24\). At \(p_a=6\): \(a_A=\frac{24}{8}=3\). At \(p_a=2\): \(a_C=\frac{24}{4}=6\). Total \(=+3\).

Hold real income fixed: the compensated bundle is still the kink bundle \((3,3)\) (the same utility; also still the unique cheapest way to reach it), so \(a_B=3\). SE \(=0\), IE \(=+3\). The L-shaped curves leave no room to substitute.

Reading the effects

When the price of \(a\) rises from \(\$2\) to \(\$3\), her consumption of \(a\) rises from 10 to 11. The substitution effect is \(-2\). What is the income effect, and what kind of good is \(a\)?

Show solution

Total \(=+1=SE+IE=-2+IE\), so \(IE=+3\). A price rise lowers real income, and she responds by buying more \(a\): \(a\) is an inferior good. The income effect (+3) outweighs the substitution effect (\(-2\)), so quantity rises with price: a Giffen good.
