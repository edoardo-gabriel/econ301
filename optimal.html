Optimal choice

Put preferences and the budget set together: the consumer picks the best bundle she can afford.

The consumer's problem

\[ \max_{a,b\ge0}\ u(a,b)\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad p_a a+p_b b\le Y. \]

Because more is better, the optimum is on the budget line. Graphically: slide up to the highest indifference curve that still touches the budget set.

The optimality condition (when it applies)

At an interior optimum, \(|\mathrm{MRS}_{ab}|=\dfrac{p_a}{p_b}\): the individual's relative value of the goods equals the market's. If \(|\mathrm{MRS}|>p_a/p_b\), she values \(a\) more than the market charges for it, so buying more \(a\) and less \(b\) makes her better off; if \(|\mathrm{MRS}|<p_a/p_b\), the reverse.

The three cases

Cobb-Douglas, \(u=a^\alpha b^\beta\)

She spends a fixed share of income on each good: \(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\) on \(a\) and \(\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\) on \(b\). Divide each expenditure by the price:

\[ a^*=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\cdot\frac{Y}{p_a},\qquad b^*=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\cdot\frac{Y}{p_b}. \]

(Derivation: set \(|\mathrm{MRS}|=\frac{\alpha b}{\beta a}=\frac{p_a}{p_b}\), solve for \(b\), and substitute into the budget line.)

Perfect complements, \(u=\min\{\alpha a,\beta b\}\)

Extra units of one good alone are wasted, so she buys at the kink, \(\alpha a=\beta b\). There is no tangency (the MRS is undefined). Combine the kink condition with the budget line:

\[ a^*=\frac{\beta Y}{\beta p_a+\alpha p_b},\qquad b^*=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\,a^*. \]

Perfect substitutes, \(u=\alpha a+\beta b\)

\(|\mathrm{MRS}|=\alpha/\beta\) is constant, so compare it with \(p_a/p_b\), or equivalently compare utility per dollar, \(\alpha/p_a\) versus \(\beta/p_b\):

  • \(\alpha/p_a>\beta/p_b\): spend everything on \(a\), \((Y/p_a,\,0)\).
  • \(\alpha/p_a<\beta/p_b\): spend everything on \(b\), \((0,\,Y/p_b)\).
  • equal: every bundle on the budget line is optimal.

This is a corner solution: the tangency condition does not hold.

Worked exercises

Handout 2, Question 3

Rob's preferences over milk \(m\) and coffee \(c\) (both measured in cups) are represented by \(u(m,c)=\min\{2m,c\}\). His budget is \(Y=\$6\), the price of milk is \(p_m=\$2\) and the price of coffee is \(p_c=\$4\). How much milk and coffee would he consume to maximize his utility?

Show solution

Perfect complements: he consumes at the kink, \(2m=c\). Substitute into the budget: \(2m+4(2m)=6\Rightarrow10m=6\Rightarrow m^*=0.6\), so \(c^*=1.2\).

Check: \(2(0.6)+4(1.2)=1.2+4.8=6\) ✓ and \(u^*=\min\{1.2,1.2\}=1.2\).

Handout 2, Question 4

Consumer B is currently consuming positive amounts of goods \(a\) and \(b\) at a point where \(|\mathrm{MRS}_{ab}|=2\). Prices are \(p_a=1\) and \(p_b=3\). Can he increase his utility? If so, how?

Show solution

Yes. He values \(a\) at 2 units of \(b\), but the market only asks \(p_a/p_b=1/3\) unit of \(b\) for it. Since \(|\mathrm{MRS}|=2>1/3\), he should buy more \(a\) and less \(b\).

Concretely: give up \(1/3\) unit of \(b\) (saves $1) to buy 1 more unit of \(a\) (costs $1). He gains about 2 units of \(b\)-equivalent value and loses only \(1/3\). He keeps doing this until \(|\mathrm{MRS}|\) falls to \(1/3\) (or he runs out of \(b\)).

Handout 2, Question 5

Consumer C's utility function is \(u(a,b)=ab^2\), and her income is \(Y\ge0\). She consumes \(a=0\). What can you say about her income if she maximizes utility? What if she does not necessarily maximize utility?

Show solution

If she maximizes utility: any bundle with \(a=0\) gives \(u=0\). If \(Y>0\), she could afford a bundle with both goods positive and \(u>0\), so \(a=0\) would not be optimal. Hence \(Y=0\).

If she doesn't necessarily maximize: nothing. She might consume \(a=0\) with any income \(Y\ge0\) (she's just not optimizing).

Working backwards

Exams often give you the answer (the optimal bundle, or the MRS at the optimum) and ask you to recover the inputs: a price, income, or the other quantity. Use the same three facts, in reverse:

  1. Optimality. At an interior optimum \(|\mathrm{MRS}_{ab}|=p_a/p_b\). For perfect complements use the kink condition instead.
  2. The MRS formula. For \(a^\alpha b^\beta\), \(|\mathrm{MRS}_{ab}|=\dfrac{\alpha b}{\beta a}\).
  3. The budget line. The optimum spends all income: \(Y=p_a a^*+p_b b^*\).

Worked example

Consumer C has \(u(a,b)=a^2b\) and faces \(p_a=4\). At the optimal choice \(a^*=3\) and \(\mathrm{MRS}_{ab}=-2\). Find \(p_b\), \(b^*\) and income \(Y\).

Show solution

Price: \(|\mathrm{MRS}|=p_a/p_b\Rightarrow p_b=4/2=2\).

Quantity: for \(a^2b\), \(|\mathrm{MRS}|=\frac{2b}{a}\). Setting \(\frac{2b}{3}=2\) gives \(b^*=3\).

Income: \(Y=4(3)+2(3)=18\). Check: Cobb-Douglas spends \(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}=\frac23\) of income on \(a\), and \(12/18=\frac23\). ✓

The same idea works with the other utility types: given the optimal bundle and prices you can find income, and given \(b^*\) for perfect complements you can find \(a^*\) from the kink.

Reading optimality from a graph

Exam graphs usually have no numbers. The slope of the indifference curve at a point is \(|\mathrm{MRS}_{ab}|\), and the slope of the budget line is \(p_a/p_b\). Compare the two slopes:

At the bundle \(A\) on the budget lineThenShe should…
curve steeper than the budget line\(|\mathrm{MRS}|>p_a/p_b\)buy more \(a\), less \(b\)
curve flatter than the budget line\(|\mathrm{MRS}|<p_a/p_b\)buy more \(b\), less \(a\)
curve just touches the line (tangent)\(|\mathrm{MRS}|=p_a/p_b\)stay: optimal (interior)
  • If the curve crosses the budget line at \(A\), a higher curve is still reachable, so \(A\) is not optimal.
  • A bundle inside the budget set leaves money unspent, so with more-is-better it is not optimal either.
  • If a question shows no indifference curve, you can't compare \(|\mathrm{MRS}|\) with \(p_a/p_b\). The answer is uncertain.
